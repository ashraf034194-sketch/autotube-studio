Step four. Eat a real breakfast, not a sugary one. A donut and a coffee will spike your blood sugar and crash it an hour later. You will be jittery, then exhausted, then hungry again. Instead, eat protein and healthy fat. Two eggs and half an avocado. Greek yogurt with nuts. A slice of whole grain toast with peanut butter. Your brain runs on stable glucose, and protein gives you that stability for hours.

Before I let you go, I want to address three common objections I hear all the time. People tell me these exact things whenever I talk about morning routines, and I want to answer them directly.

Objection one. I am not a morning person. I have tried waking up early and it just makes me miserable. Here is the truth. Nobody is naturally a morning person. Waking up early is a habit, and like every habit, it feels terrible for the first ten days, uncomfortable for the next ten, and then it becomes your new normal. Your body is incredibly adaptable. Give it two weeks and your circadian rhythm will adjust. The key is consistency, not the specific time on the clock. Wake up at the same hour every single day, including weekends, and your body will figure it out.

Objection two. I have kids. My mornings are chaos. I hear you. I really do. If you have young children, your morning is largely shaped by their needs. But here is what I would say. You can still control the first ten minutes. Keep a glass of water next to your bed. Drink it the moment you wake up, before you respond to the first cry or the first request. That ten seconds of intentional action sets a tiny anchor. The rest of the routine might be hijacked by children, but you still started the day on your terms. Build around that anchor.

Objection three. I work night shifts. My mornings are at four in the afternoon. This is fine. The routine does not care about the clock. It cares about the transition from sleep to wake. Whenever you wake up, drink the water, get the light, move your body, eat something real, do one focused thing. The order is the same. The biology is the same. The time on the wall is irrelevant.

Now let me give you a bonus tip that most people miss. Your evening routine is actually the foundation of your morning routine. You cannot wake up hydrated if you went to bed dehydrated. You cannot wake up energized if you scrolled your phone until two in the morning. You cannot wake up calm if you went to bed angry about something you saw on the news. The last hour of your day determines the first hour of your next day. So if you want a better morning, design a better evening. Dim the lights an hour before bed. Put your phone in another room. Read a few pages of a physical book. Write down three things you are grateful for. Then sleep.

A quick note on coffee. I love coffee. I am not telling you to quit coffee. But here is the rule. Do not drink coffee in the first ninety minutes after waking up. Your cortisol naturally spikes in the morning, and if you drink coffee on top of that spike, you build tolerance faster and you crash harder in the afternoon. Wait ninety minutes. Then brew your coffee. You will get more energy from the same cup, and you will not hit the three pm wall. Try it for a week and you will feel the difference.

Let me also say something about weekends. Most people crush their morning routine Monday through Friday and then completely abandon it on Saturday and Sunday. They sleep in until noon, they skip breakfast, they scroll for an hour. Then Monday morning feels like a jet lag. Their body is confused. Their rhythm is broken. They spend Monday and Tuesday just getting back to baseline. If you want the routine to stick, you have to do it on weekends too. Not as strictly, maybe. You can push the wake time back by an hour on weekends. But the structure stays. Water, light, movement, food, focus. Five things. Every day. Seven days a week.
